CiviLedger: a verification business with a blockchain underneath

A plan to turn the BCOLBD 2026 concept into something an institution will pay for within twelve months — starting with academic and professional credentials, with the consortium ledger acting as the integrity and revocation substrate rather than the product.

Team HexaMind / 2HAAS — United International University Version 1.0 — planning baseline Status pre-build

01How to use this file

This document is the shared context for the CiviLedger build. It is written for two readers: the team, and Claude Code when it is asked to implement any part of the system.

  • Binding sections — §6 (blockchain's job), §7 (architecture), §8–§15 (technical specifications), §25 (invariants), §26 (non-goals). Do not deviate without editing this file first and noting the change.
  • Indicative sections — §19–§23 (market, pricing, roadmap). These carry assumptions that the pilot is supposed to falsify. Treat numbers here as hypotheses, not facts.
  • When implementing, read §25 first. Those rules are the ones that, if broken, make the system illegal, unsafe, or pointless.
  • Keep a short CLAUDE.md at the repo root that says: "Read PLAN.html §7, §25, §26 before making architectural decisions." Do not duplicate this file's content there; duplication causes drift.
Honesty clause

The BCOLBD whitepaper was written to score points against a rubric, and it does that well. This plan is written to survive contact with a paying customer, which is a different test. Where the two disagree, this file wins — and §3 lists every disagreement explicitly so nothing is quietly dropped.

02Thesis

Nobody buys a trust layer. People buy an answer to "is this certificate real?" delivered in seconds instead of weeks. Sell the answer; build the trust layer underneath it because that is the only way the answer stays trustworthy at scale.

Three sentences that should survive every rewrite of the pitch:

  1. The wedge is academic and professional credential verification for hiring and migration — not national identity. Identity verification in Bangladesh is already served by the Election Commission's Porichoy gateway; academic verification is not served, and still takes one to three weeks of manual board work.
  2. The first issuers are private universities, not ministries. A private university owns its own records and can sign a memorandum of understanding without an act of parliament. UIU is the design partner. That is the team's genuine unfair advantage and it should be spent immediately.
  3. The blockchain is invisible to every user and exists to answer one question a database cannot: how does a verifier know that CiviLedger itself did not forge this credential? Everything else in the architecture follows from that question.

If, eighteen months from now, three universities issue through us, a hundred employers verify through us, and nobody in either group has ever said the word "blockchain," the project has succeeded.

03What changes from the whitepaper

Every row below is a deliberate departure. The left column is not wrong — it is competition-optimal. The right column is business-optimal.

AreaWhitepaper positionPlan position, and why
Scope Identity + academic + transport credentials, three simulated authorities, national ambition. One credential family — academic and professional — issued by institutions we can actually sign. Transport and NID are roadmap, not v1. Three domains at once means three sales cycles, three schemas and three regulators before a single taka of revenue.
Identity An "Identity Authority" node issues NID-equivalent credentials. We never issue identity. We consume identity from Porichoy or from passport + face match, and we record the assurance level we achieved. Building a competing identity authority is a fight with the Election Commission that we lose by default.
Who holds the wallet Citizen wallet with device-bound keys is the centre of the design. The wallet is real but it is not the wedge. Consumer wallet adoption is the hardest distribution problem in the world. v1 ships a custodial web wallet plus a shareable verification link; self-custody is an upgrade path for users who want it, not a prerequisite for anyone.
Revocation CredentialStatus.sol with per-credential suspend() / revoke() writes. W3C Bitstring Status List: one bit per credential in an off-chain list, whose hash is anchored on-chain. One transaction covers 131,072 credentials, revocation is instant, and a verifier can cache the list and check status offline.
Consent/audit ConsentAudit.recordPresentationReceipt() writes every presentation to chain. Per-presentation on-chain writes publish a verification graph — who checked whom, when — which is the single most sensitive dataset the system will ever hold, and it does not scale. Receipts are stored off-chain and a daily Merkle root is anchored. Same tamper-evidence, none of the leakage.
Selective disclosure "Purpose-specific minimal credentials" — the issuer pre-signs a narrow variant. SD-JWT VC. Real selective disclosure with salted digests, one credential, holder chooses which claims to reveal at presentation time. It is an IETF specification with working JavaScript libraries, it is what the EU digital identity wallet uses, and it removes the combinatorial mess of pre-signing a variant per use case.
Protocols Bespoke QR and REST flows. OpenID4VCI for issuance, OpenID4VP for presentation. Standard flows mean a foreign employer's existing wallet or verifier can talk to us without a custom integration — which is exactly the credibility we lack.
Consortium Three validator organisations, one per authority. Same target, honest sequencing. Day one, three nodes exist but two are operated by us — that is not a consortium and we will not claim it is. We publish who runs each node, hand node two to the design-partner university and node three to an independent operator as soon as they exist, and checkpoint the chain's state root to a public network weekly so that even total collusion is externally detectable.
Legacy records Not addressed. Issuance assumes new credentials. Retroactive attestation is the day-one feature. Almost every certificate anyone needs verified in the next decade was issued on paper before we existed. A system that only serves the class of 2027 is useless until 2032. See §5.2.
Issuer incentive "Lower manual verification burden." Too soft. Universities already charge students for verification and attestation. We give issuers a revenue share on verification fees, so joining is income, not an IT project. See §20.2.
Revenue Membership fees, API tiers, integration services. Same three lines, different order. Verification fees first (verifiers have budget today), citizen migration bundles second (consumers already pay attestation agencies thousands of taka), membership last — it only becomes credible once an association exists.

04The problem we sell against

4.1 The bottleneck is the first link, not the last

Bangladesh acceded to the Hague Apostille Convention in July 2024 and launched an e-Apostille system from March 2025: a digitally signed, QR-verifiable certificate from the Ministry of Foreign Affairs, applied for through myGov, accepted in 120+ countries. The government's own estimate of the saving is on the order of BDT 700 crore a year.

That fixes the last link in the chain. It does not touch the first one. Before MOFA will apostille an academic certificate, the issuing education board or university must verify it — and that step commonly takes one to three weeks, is the least predictable part of the process, slows further around result publication and intake seasons, and is where name mismatches surface for the first time. Degree certificates do not go to a board at all: public universities verify their own, National University verifies its affiliated colleges, and private universities have an extra step.

Positioning

CiviLedger makes the slowest link instant, and produces an artefact the fast link can consume. We are not competing with e-Apostille; we are feeding it. The same signed credential serves the domestic employer who never needed an apostille at all.

4.3 The fraud that actually happens

"Forged certificate" is only one of four failure modes, and the system must address all four or it will be sold on a promise it cannot keep.

  1. Outright forgery — a fabricated document. Solved by cryptographic issuance.
  2. A real certificate presented by the wrong person. Solved by holder binding, not by signatures. This is why §13 exists.
  3. Altered grades or a real certificate from an unaccredited institution. Solved by signing the full transcript rather than the certificate alone, and by carrying accreditation status in the issuer registry.
  4. Fabricated experience letters and training certificates. Not solved in v1 — it is the v2 credential type, and it is where the next revenue line sits.

05The product

5.1 Three surfaces

SurfaceUserWhat it does
Issuer Console University registrar / exam controller Import records from the existing student information system (CSV or adapter), review and approve a batch, sign it, publish. Revoke or supersede with one click. A dashboard showing verifications answered, staff-hours avoided, and revenue share accrued — the retention feature, and the thing the registrar shows their vice-chancellor.
Verification page & API Employer, agency, foreign institution Scan a QR, paste a link, or upload a PDF; get a result in under two seconds: issuer, accreditation, integrity, validity window, revocation status, holder-binding assurance. Produces a signed, downloadable verification report that a third party can independently check. REST API and webhooks for volume users.
Credential wallet Graduate Progressive web app first. Holds credentials, generates time-boxed share links with a chosen claim scope, and shows a log of every verification performed against them. Self-custody keys are an opt-in upgrade, not a requirement.

A fourth internal surface — the governance console for issuer onboarding, key rotation ceremonies and oversight read-only access — is operations tooling, not a product. Build it plainly.

5.2 The feature that makes it useful on day one

Retroactive attestation. The registrar selects an existing alumni record from the period 1998–2026, the system checks it against the institution's system of record, and the institution signs a credential asserting this record exists in our registry, verified by this method, on this date. The credential carries an assuranceLevel and an evidence block describing exactly how the check was performed.

Design consequence

Assurance level is a first-class field on every credential, surfaced in every verification result. A verifier must be able to distinguish "born digital, signed at issuance from the system of record" from "a registrar confirmed a scanned certificate against a ledger book in 2027." Hiding that distinction would make us the fraud.

06Where blockchain earns its place

This section exists because the most damaging question anyone can ask is "why not a Postgres table with digital signatures?" — and it is a good question. Signed credentials alone get you most of the value. Here is the part they do not get, stated as four jobs.

JobWhat breaks without a ledgerContract
1. Issuer key registry If CiviLedger Ltd. controls the mapping from institution to signing key, CiviLedger Ltd. can mint a degree from any university and no verifier can tell. This is the load-bearing argument for the whole design. Key registration and rotation must be a governed, append-only, externally auditable event. IssuerRegistry
2. Revocation that cannot be quietly undone A centralised status service can un-revoke a credential, or serve one answer to one verifier and a different answer to another. Anchoring the status list hash with a monotonic version makes both detectable. StatusRegistry
3. Proof of existence at a point in time Protects the graduate as much as the verifier: an institution cannot retroactively deny or edit what it signed, and we cannot backdate a credential into history. AnchorRegistry
4. Governance and audit trail An oversight body needs a log of admissions, suspensions and policy changes that the operator cannot edit. Administrator-editable logs are not audit. Governance, AuditAnchor

6.1 The falsifiable test

For any proposed on-chain write, ask: would a rational verifier's trust decision change if the operator could silently alter this record? If yes, it belongs on chain. If no, it belongs in Postgres. Apply this to every new feature.

6.2 What is never on chain

  • Names, national ID numbers, dates of birth, addresses, phone numbers, email addresses, photographs, biometrics.
  • Grades, transcripts, claim values of any kind.
  • Unsalted hashes of any of the above. A hash of a small-domain value is a lookup table away from being the value; we treat it as personal data and keep it off chain.
  • Stable per-citizen identifiers. No citizen address, no citizen DID, nothing that lets an observer link two credentials to the same person.
  • Individual verification events.
Erasure

Under the Personal Data Protection Act 2026 a data subject can demand erasure. An immutable ledger cannot honour that. We resolve the conflict by making sure nothing on the ledger is personal data in the first place: every anchored digest is salted with a per-credential secret held off chain, so destroying the off-chain record renders the on-chain digest an unlinkable random value. Erasure is implemented as key and salt destruction. See §18.

08Credential format

SD-JWT VC. The issuer signs a JWT whose selectively-disclosable claims are replaced by salted digests; the holder forwards only the disclosures they choose. Verifier recomputes digests and matches.

// Issuer payload (before disclosure processing)
{
  "iss": "https://civiledger.com.bd/issuers/uiu",
  "vct": "bd.civiledger.academic.degree.v1",
  "iat": 1790000000,
  "exp": null,                       // degrees do not expire; licences do
  "cnf": { "jwk": { ... } },         // holder binding key
  "status": {
    "status_list": { "idx": 41297, "uri": ".../status/uiu/3" }
  },
  "_sd_alg": "sha-256",
  "_sd": [ "<digest>", "<digest>", ... ],  // selectively disclosable claims

  // always-visible claims
  "issuer_name": "United International University",
  "accreditation": "UGC_RECOGNIZED",
  "assurance_level": "AL2",
  "evidence": {
    "method": "REGISTRAR_SOURCE_CHECK",
    "source_system": "UIU SIS",
    "checked_at": "2027-02-11",
    "checked_by_role": "EXAM_CONTROLLER"
  }
}

// Selectively disclosable claim set for vct = academic.degree.v1
//   given_name, family_name, name_bn, date_of_birth, national_id_last4,
//   programme, degree_level, major, result_cgpa, result_class,
//   graduation_date, registration_no, transcript_digest, photo_digest
Schema rule

Every vct is versioned and immutable once published. A schema change means a new vct and a migration note in the repo — never an edit in place. Verifiers in other countries will pin against these types and we cannot break them.

Credential types for v1 and their sequencing:

vctPhaseNotes
bd.civiledger.academic.degree.v11The anchor product. Includes transcript digest.
bd.civiledger.academic.transcript.v11Separate credential so a verifier can request grades only when justified.
bd.civiledger.academic.enrolment.v12Current-student proof — the highest-frequency, lowest-stakes credential; good for volume.
bd.civiledger.professional.licence.v12Expiry and revocation genuinely matter here, which is where status lists shine.

09Revocation & status

W3C Bitstring Status List. Each issuer maintains one or more lists; each credential holds an index into a list. A list is a bitstring, gzipped, published as a signed JWT at a stable URL, with its hash and a monotonically increasing version recorded on chain.

Status list
  size        131,072 entries (16 KB uncompressed, ~2 KB gzipped typical)
  values      0 = valid, 1 = revoked
  second list per issuer for "suspended" where the type needs it
  published   https://civiledger.com.bd/status/{orgId}/{listId}
  anchored    StatusRegistry.publishStatusList(orgId, listId, hash, uri, version)

Revocation path (target: under 60 seconds end to end)
  1. registrar clicks revoke, gives a reason code
  2. bit flipped, new list built and signed
  3. list published to CDN
  4. publishStatusList() with version + 1
  5. cache-control 1h; verifiers re-fetch and compare hash against chain

Index allocation
  - random allocation within the list, never sequential by graduation order
  - sequential indices leak cohort and issuance order to anyone holding the list
  - a fresh list is started at 80% occupancy
Rollback defence

A verifier that has seen version n must reject a served list whose on-chain version is below n. Without this check an operator with CDN access could serve a stale list to suppress a revocation. Implement it in the shared verifier library, not in each client.

10Consent & audit

Every presentation produces a consent receipt: which credential, which claims, which verifier, which purpose, when, under what legal basis. Receipts live in Postgres. Each night a Merkle tree is built over the day's receipt digests and the root is anchored via AuditAnchor.

  • The graduate sees their own receipts in the wallet — a genuine differentiator and a direct answer to the PDPA's transparency obligations.
  • The issuer sees aggregate counts, never verifier identity per graduate, unless the graduate opts in.
  • An oversight body can be given an inclusion proof for any receipt without being given the receipt database.
  • Retention: receipts expire on a defined schedule (propose 24 months) and expiry is enforced by a job, not by policy prose.

11Smart contracts

Five contracts, all registries, no value transfer, no token, no transfer function anywhere. Target under 600 lines of Solidity in total. If a contract starts growing logic, that logic almost certainly belongs in the application layer.

// Governance.sol — OpenZeppelin AccessControl
// ---------------------------------------------------------------
// Roles: ADMIN, ISSUER, ORACLE (anchor worker), OVERSIGHT (read-only)
// Writes to IssuerRegistry require an M-of-N approval from ADMIN holders
// held by distinct organisations. One organisation must never be able to

function proposeAction(bytes32 actionHash, bytes calldata payload) external;

Contract rules
  • No transfer, approve, mint, or ERC-20/721/1155 interface anywhere. Credentials are not assets.
  • No string PII in any parameter or event. Reviewers should grep for string and justify each one.
  • Every state change emits an event; the indexer reads events, never storage.
  • Upgradeability: none in v1. Deploy new contracts and migrate the registry by governance action. A proxy on the contract that defines institutional trust is a governance hole.
  • Test coverage floor: 95% lines on IssuerRegistry and StatusRegistry, including fuzz tests on keyAt across rotation boundaries.

12Keys, recovery, offline

12.1 Issuer keys

Non-exportable, in KMS or an HSM, one key per issuer, never shared across issuers. Rotation is a scheduled ceremony with two people present and a governance transaction. A compromised key is revoked with a reason and an effective timestamp; credentials signed before the compromise window remain valid, which is precisely why keyAt() takes a timestamp.

12.2 Holder keys

The whitepaper's device-bound-only model loses credentials whenever a phone is lost, which in this market is often. Three tiers, chosen by the user:

TierKey custodyRecovery
Hosted (default)Server-side, per-user encryptedAccount recovery via issuer re-identification. Lowest friction, adequate for v1.
Device-boundPlatform keystore (WebAuthn / Secure Enclave)Encrypted backup blob, user passphrase, plus issuer-assisted re-issuance.
Self-custodyUser-held key, exportableUser's responsibility, with a loud warning. Offered because some institutional users will demand it.

Loss recovery in all tiers ends the same way: re-identify the person, revoke the old holder binding, re-issue against a new key. Build that path in week one; it is the most-used support flow in any credential system.

12.3 Offline verification

A district office with intermittent connectivity must still be able to verify. The verifier app caches a signed issuer-registry snapshot and the relevant status lists, both with published freshness windows. Verification results state their basis: verified against registry snapshot of 14 March, status list version 212. Never present a cached result as a live one.

13Identity binding

A signature proves a credential is authentic. It does not prove the person presenting it is the subject. Without binding we ship a better-looking version of the problem we claim to solve.

LevelHow the subject was identifiedWhere it applies
AL0Self-asserted; no check.Never accepted for a published credential. Exists so the code has a floor value.
AL1Document scan plus OCR, name matched against the institutional record.Bulk backfill of old alumni where no in-person step is possible.
AL2Institutional staff confirmed the record against the system of record and matched the person to it.The default for retroactive attestation.
AL3Born-digital issuance from the system of record at graduation, holder key bound at enrolment.The target steady state for every new cohort.
AL3+AL3 plus a live NID or passport check with face match at wallet enrolment.Required for regulated verifiers; uses Porichoy where we are licensed, otherwise passport plus liveness.

Porichoy is licensed to Bangladesh Bank–regulated entities and approved payment service operators under bilateral agreements, so direct access is not available to us at the start. Design the identity provider as a swappable adapter — PorichoyAdapter, PassportMRZAdapter, PartnerBankAdapter — and note that partnering with a regulated entity is a legitimate route to the first one.

Data rule

No table joins a subject to a ledger identifier. The link between a person and an on-chain anchor exists only through credentials.salt_enc, and destroying that row severs it permanently. This is the mechanism that makes erasure possible; do not add a convenience column that defeats it.

16Trust bootstrap

The hardest problem is not technical. A recruiter in Dubai has no reason to trust a domain they have never heard of. Five moves, in order of cost:

  1. Open the verifier. Publish the verification specification and an MIT-licensed JavaScript library so anyone can verify a CiviLedger credential without touching our servers. A verification that depends on our uptime and honesty is not verification.
  2. Publish the registry. The issuer registry, its chain proofs, and node operator identities are public. Anyone can audit who can sign what.
  3. Signed reports. Every verification produces a report that carries its own signature and proofs — forwardable, checkable, and usable as evidence in a compliance file.
  4. Borrow institutional credibility. UGC recognition status in the registry; an MoU published by the partner university; a named independent node operator; an oversight seat for a law firm or professional body before anyone asks for one.
  5. Feed the apostille chain. Work toward the issuer's digital attestation being accepted as the board/university verification step that precedes MOFA's e-Apostille. Slow, political, and worth more than every other item combined.

18Legal & compliance

Bangladesh gazetted the Personal Data Protection Ordinance in November 2025, amended it in February 2026, and Parliament passed the Personal Data Protection Act 2026 in April 2026. This is no longer a future consideration — it is the operating environment, with a compliance window running from gazette publication.

ObligationHow the design answers it
Consent before collection and useConsent receipts with purpose and claim scope, captured at presentation, visible to the subject.
Data classification and localisationCredential claims and identity data are treated as confidential or restricted and stored in Bangladesh. This is a hosting decision, not a preference — make it before the first production deploy.
Erasure and rectificationImplemented as destruction of the off-chain record, salt and key. The on-chain digest becomes an unlinkable random value. Rectification is supersession: revoke and re-issue, never edit.
Purpose limitation and minimisationSelective disclosure at the protocol level; verifiers request a claim scope and the server refuses claims outside it.
Cross-border transferNo claim data leaves the country in v1. Any future foreign verifier integration receives a verification result, not a data export.
AccountabilityNamed data protection officer, records of processing, DPIA before the pilot handles real graduate data.
Before real data

Do not touch a single real graduate record until: a signed data-processing agreement with the issuing institution exists, a DPIA is written, hosting is in-country, and a lawyer has read both. The prototype runs on synthetic data — generate it, do not borrow it.

Also in scope, to be checked with counsel: the Cyber Security Ordinance 2025 and the National Data Governance Ordinance 2025; the legal weight of an electronic signature for institutional attestation; and whether any UGC or ministry approval is needed before a private university's digital attestation can substitute for its paper one.

19Go to market

19.1 Sequencing

Each stage exists to buy the right to attempt the next one.

  1. One university, one department. UIU. Backfill five years of one department's graduates. Free. The goal is a working system and a reference, not revenue.
  2. Three employers. Alumni employers first — warm introductions from the same university. Free tier. The goal is the turnaround-time number that becomes the whole sales pitch.
  3. Two more private universities. The pitch is now a demonstration plus a revenue share, not a concept. Private universities compete on graduate employability; "our degrees verify in two seconds" is a marketing asset for them.
  4. Paid verifiers. Recruitment and migration agencies, then background-check firms, then bank and hospital HR. They have budget lines already.
  5. Professional bodies. Licences with expiry and revocation — the credential type where our status-list design is obviously better than a PDF.
  6. The public side. Education boards, National University, UGC. Only attempt this with commercial traction behind it. Government adoption follows demonstrated private-sector use far more reliably than it precedes it.

19.2 What a university actually needs to hear

Not a blockchain pitch. In order: we will not touch your database; your registrar keeps the final say on every batch; your signing key is yours; your verification workload drops; you earn a share of every verification fee; and here is another university already doing it. The last item is why step one matters more than steps three through six.

20.2 The revenue share

A defined share of every verification fee earned against an institution's credentials flows back to that institution, tracked in organizations.revenue_share_bps and reported in the issuer dashboard. This converts the sales conversation from "please adopt our system" to "here is a new income line on records you already hold," and it makes the issuer an active participant in recruiting verifiers. Set the share generously at first; it is cheaper than a sales team.

20.3 Unit economics — a frame, not a forecast

Fill these in from pilot data. The point of writing them now is to know which measurements the pilot must produce.

Revenue per verification              R    (price, net of revenue share)
Marginal cost per verification        C    (compute + storage + support)
Verifications per credential per year V    (the reuse factor — the key ratio)
Credentials issued per institution    N
Cost to onboard one institution       K    (integration + backfill + legal)

Annual gross margin per institution   = N × V × (R − C)
Payback period on an institution      = K / (N × V × (R − C))

Sensitivity: the business lives or dies on V. One verification per
credential per year makes this a services company. Five or more makes
it infrastructure. Instrument V from the first week of the pilot.

20.4 Funding path

Competition prize money and university support cover the prototype. The pilot needs a grant or a small pre-seed; ICT Division innovation funds, startup programmes, and donor-funded migration or skills programmes are all plausible and all move slowly, so start the applications during the prototype phase rather than after it.

25Hard invariants

Never violate
  1. No personal data on chain — including unsalted hashes of personal data, and including in event parameters.
  2. No per-presentation on-chain writes. Ever. The verification graph stays off chain.
  3. No stable per-citizen on-chain identifier.
  4. Verification must be possible with only: the credential, a signed registry snapshot, and a status list. If a check requires calling our API, the design is wrong.
  5. No transfer, mint, or token semantics in any contract. Credentials are non-transferable records.
  6. keyAt() resolves keys by signing timestamp, not by current key. A rotation must never invalidate history.
  7. Status-list versions are monotonic and clients reject any regression.
  8. Assurance level and evidence are surfaced in every verification result. Never present AL1 as if it were AL3.
  9. Issuer signing keys are non-exportable and never held by CiviLedger in a unilaterally usable form.
  10. Claim scope is enforced server-side. A verifier cannot receive a claim it did not request and justify.
  11. Real personal data requires a signed DPA, an in-country deployment and a DPIA first. Synthetic data until then.
  12. A cached verification result is always labelled as cached, with its freshness window.

26Non-goals

  • Not an identity provider. We consume identity; we never assert it.
  • Not a document store. We hold what is needed to issue and verify, not a citizen's filing cabinet.
  • No token, no coin, no transferable credential, no financialisation of identity. Not in v1, not in v5.
  • No public chain for credential data. Public checkpointing of state roots only.
  • No zero-knowledge circuits in v1. SD-JWT covers the real disclosure requirements. Revisit only when a customer requirement cannot be met without it.
  • No native mobile apps before the PWA has users.
  • No national rollout narrative in sales material. Sell the verification that works today.
  • No custom cryptography. If a design needs a novel scheme, the design is wrong.

27Open questions

These need answers from the team or from outside; they are not implementation details and should not be guessed at in code.

  1. Which university signs first, and who inside it owns the decision — registrar, exam controller, or vice-chancellor's office?
  2. What does that university's system of record actually look like, and can it be read programmatically or only exported?
  3. Does a private university's digital attestation currently satisfy the board or ministry step in the apostille chain, and if not, who decides that?
  4. What do employers and agencies pay today, per verification, including staff time? This number is the price ceiling and nobody has measured it.
  5. Which regulated partner could give us a lawful route to Porichoy, and what do they want in return?
  6. Who operates node three, and are they willing to be named publicly?
  7. What legal entity holds the contracts, and what is the ownership split among six founders? Settle this before revenue, not after.
  8. Which public network for checkpointing, and who pays its fees in year three?
